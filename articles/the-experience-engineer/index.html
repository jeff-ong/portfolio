The Experience Engineer

October 30, 2026

Nothing is forever. We have to acknowledge that AI has brought some irreversible changes to the world, good and bad ones. Our title as a software engineer has to change, because our job has changed.

TLDR

  • An Experience Engineer is a software engineer who builds with an AI assistant. The output is the user experience.
  • The role is 80% engineering and 20% UX.
  • The assistant works at build time and drafts the surface.
  • The engineer keeps what cannot be vibecoded: governance, infrastructure, and experience.
  • Intent architecture settles what the product promised, so the assistant and the runtime do not renegotiate it.
  • Trust cannot be copied off a public product, and it does not travel with a personal agent.
  • A superplatform may rent that machinery. The experience still has to tell the truth for someone who was not in the room.

The jobs moved

AI development is uncertain. The tools change faster than the titles, and the work under the titles changes faster than either. What a team should hire for gets rewritten every few months.

The jobs pivoted in place. Each one took on more than the title was built for. The marketer produces the campaign. The designer ships the interface. The engineer decides what the product should be. The old boundaries kept each kind of work honest. The boundaries are gone. The honesty did not come with the extra scope.

Marketing shows it most clearly. The deliverables are subjective: a line, a landing page, a deck, a cut of a campaign. They are judged by feel, and rarely checked for accuracy. The assistant is fluent at exactly that. It writes copy that sounds like the brand, and claims nobody checked. When nothing asks whether it is true, AI slop is the rational output. I see it in the marketing around the products I build. The pages look finished. Fewer of them had to survive a standard.

Product work gets less mercy, because someone uses it. A wrong state, a lost save, a confident answer with nothing behind it: those show up as the experience. A role is forming here, and it still does not have a stable name.

A name for the work

When the title fits everyone, it describes the tools. The job is still the product.

Companies post it as a design engineer, a UX engineer, sometimes an AI experience engineer. This year Precisely asked for an Experience Engineer who would take a product from the first sketch to a tested, shipped interface, using AI along the way. J.D. Power asked for an AI Experience Engineer: a full-stack engineer who owns the human-facing layer. The titles are wandering. The work is settling.

An Experience Engineer is a software engineer who builds with an AI assistant. The output is the user experience. The role is 80% engineering and 20% UX. Design and development are one effort. Engineering is the hard layer and the runtime. UX is knowing when the experience is wrong. The difference from the marketing failure is the standard.

That job used to be split so the standard could be staffed. Research became a spec, the spec a picture, the picture tickets, the tickets a pull request that almost matched the picture. Each handoff thinned the experience. We called the distance process.

The split made sense when building was expensive. A wrong screen cost a sprint.

Building is cheap now. You can describe a screen, throw it away, and generate another before lunch. The cost that stayed is judgment. Left alone, that is the marketing condition: fluent output, light scrutiny, slop.

The assistant takes what can be vibecoded. Screens, copy, the first shape of a flow. That work got cheap. It should stay with the assistant.

Intent architecture

Understanding the intent remains the most crucial part of software development, and of solving any problem. The problem is the most important piece. A screen, a platform, an assistant: each of them comes after that.

The assistant can draft a screen from a sentence. It cannot tell which sentence was the product.

Intent is what a person is trying to do, and what the product agreed to do back. Architecture is how that agreement is held: through a new draft, a rented platform, and a person who was not in the room.

Settle the promises while the product is being made. A payment happens once. The session belongs to one person. The account can be left. The stream keeps the write. The screen may say those things. It may not invent a different agreement because a draft sounded finished, or a platform default was close enough.

Without that, the build stays a conversation. The assistant fills the gap with something plausible. The platform fills it with something familiar. The user arrives, and the product is still deciding.

Governance, infrastructure, and the experience are where the intent has to hold. Governance is the promise, written so it can be checked. Infrastructure is the path that keeps it when the click happens again. The experience is what a person can tell from the outside. If one drifts, the other two describe a product you do not have.

The work that cannot be vibecoded

An assistant can draft the screen for each of these in an afternoon. It cannot be accountable once someone depends on it.

A payment gateway

The checkout can look finished and still be a lie. The button says the payment went through. The webhook arrives twice, and the person is charged twice. Or the charge lands and the thing they paid for never unlocks. A payment has to happen once, reach the right account, survive a retry, and be reversible when the product was wrong. The assistant can draw the button. The engineer keeps the path the money takes.

Authentication

A login form is easy. Authentication is the product knowing who is there. The session expires when it should. Recovery cannot be used by the next person. Looking logged in is the surface. Being logged in is infrastructure. Every later promise depends on having the right person.

Account management

An account is that person over time: what they can change, export, and leave, and who else can see the work. A personal agent has one account, the author. A product has many, and each has to work without the author in the room. A workspace with a login screen is still a workspace. An account someone can recover and close is a product.

Scale and the stream

The experience has to hold when that person is no longer the only one. A queue that does not drop the write. A stream that stays in order. A system that stays honest when a hundred people do what the author did once on a laptop. The demo can be vibecoded. The path the data takes cannot. A runtime that asks an agent to reconsider every event will not hold under load. The stream should carry what the build already decided.

Trust is what that feels like from the outside. The charge matches the agreement. The session is theirs. The account is recoverable. The product still behaves when they are not the only user.

The superplatform

Those four pieces are the prize the major platforms are fighting over.

A superplatform is one provider where a person can do everything, and the product behind them never has to leave. Payment, sign-in, account, and the stream stop being four vendors. They fold into one service. The builder is no longer asked to assemble what they cannot vibecode.

Payments companies want the identity, not only the card. The clouds already run the stream, and they want the login and the bill. The AI platforms want the workspace, and a customer who never opens another tab. Whoever holds that layer holds the part a weekend clone cannot fake, and a personal agent cannot export.

Rent the hard parts. Spend the build on the experience. A stranger can rely on the product before you become a payments company, an identity company, and a data company.

The paths can be rented. The truth stays with the product. The platform does not know what you promised. A familiar badge can make the surface look finished the way a generated screen does. The charge can be correct while the thing they thought they bought never unlocks. The superplatform sells the machinery. You still keep whether it is telling the truth here.

The rented features will converge. The experience on top of them will not. Two products can share the same gateway, sign-in, account, and stream, and only one will deserve to be trusted. Remove the logo: does it still behave. Remove the engineer: does anyone know when it does not.

Trust cannot be copied

You still cannot copy that machinery off a public screen.

People are running fast personal workspaces. Custom tools. Agents that know one repo, one inbox, one way of working. The agent is fast because the context is private. Trust is easy when the only user wrote the instructions and checks the output.

Sharing that agent is a different problem from having it. The context does not travel. Put it in front of someone else and the failures become the product: the wrong account, a tool call they cannot see, a step that depended on taste nobody wrote down. You can export the prompts. The trust stays where it was made.

The same limit shows up when a product is cloned in public. The interface is visible. With an assistant, a plausible checkout, login, and settings page is an afternoon, and a shared platform makes the clone cheaper still. What the public product does not give you is why it holds: the charge that fires once, the session that ends on time, the account that can be closed, the stream that lands when the room is full. Trust cannot be reverse-engineered. It was never on the screen, and it is not in the platform fee.

Tokens belong at build time

Agents belong at build time. While the product is being made, the assistant can wander, draft ten versions, and throw them away. One person is waiting. The wasted tokens are the cost of deciding. The flow, the refusals, and the steps that can be ordinary code get settled while you are building.

Runtime is a different budget. Every token is paid again, per user. An agent that re-plans a step you already settled is a double charge, a session that should have ended, or a late event treated as new. The product gets slower and less trustworthy in proportion to how much thinking you left until the click. A platform that hosts the agent has a reason to leave that loop running. The meter is theirs.

Token minimizing means using less at runtime. If a path can be code, a query, a cache, or a fixed step, it should already be that. A model call stays only where a judgment is still required, and the context stays small. By the time a second person shows up, the open loop should be over.

What you can charge for

A personal agent is hard to share and hard to charge for. Ship it without a build step and every customer inherits the open loop: the tokens, the wait, and decisions there was time to make once. They also inherit the missing pieces, unless you rented them on purpose.

The workshop is fast because you are in it. You bring the context, catch the mistake, and pay the tokens while the work is still being decided. Sharing it hands someone your bench and your bill. Selling the agent asks the buyer to become the author.

The product is the runtime you will stand behind. The assistant already spent what it needed at build time. The experience has to do one specific thing, for someone who was not in the room, at a cost you can repeat. That is what can be shared. Trust is why they come back. It cannot be attached to the export afterward.

Where the role starts

Nearby roles cover part of this. They start from different places.

A UX engineer serves a design decision: prototypes, the design system, the last mile into production. The decision sits with the design team.

A design engineer starts from design and ships that decision in a pull request.

An experience engineer starts from software engineering. Design and implementation are one loop, and much of the surface is done with an assistant. The measure is the experience in production, including what is under the screen. Can someone pay, sign in, and keep what they made. Does it hold when more people arrive. About four fifths of the work is engineering. The remaining fifth is UX: knowing when the experience is wrong.

I have worked both sides of the old split. I studied computer science and interface design in the same stretch of school, then spent years building other people’s interfaces: global sites, design systems, clinical 3D tools. Engineering showed me where experiences break. An empty state. A click a few hundred milliseconds too slow. A payment that reported success and was not success. A session that belonged to the wrong person. Design showed me that those breaks are the product. A correct-looking system nobody can trust is unfinished.

AI did not erase either lesson. It removed the excuse that one person cannot hold both. The assistant can produce the surface all day. A platform can rent the gateway, the account, and the stream. Neither knows which version is telling the truth for this user. That difference is the job.

The test

Marketing shows the new responsibility with no standard of accuracy. A public clone shows a standard made of whatever could be seen from outside. A personal agent shows one person’s tolerance and an open token bill. A superplatform shows the hard parts for rent, and a logo standing in for the trust.

The Experience Engineer keeps a harder standard. The assistant works at build time. The platform can carry the payment, the session, the account, and the stream. The engineer keeps the experience those pieces make.

There will be more Platform Dependent Engineers. Their ability to do the job depends solely on a platform: OpenAI, Anthropic Claude, and the others that follow. Take the platform away, and the work stops.

Take the assistant away: can this person still engineer the product? Take the platform away: do they still know which promises were theirs? Hand it to someone who did not build it: can that person pay once, sign in as themselves, keep the account, and trust it while others do the same? The Experience Engineer is the person who has to answer yes.
